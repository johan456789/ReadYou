Section 1: Getting started

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog near the riverbank at dawn. Birds gather on the telephone wires while the town slowly wakes up. Coffee steam rises from cups on windowsills.

Meanwhile the fox trots along the hedgerow, nose to the ground, following last night's scent trail through the damp grass and fallen leaves.

Section 2: The middle of the morning

By nine o'clock the market square is busy. Stalls sell apples, cheese, bread, and flowers. Children chase pigeons between the fountain and the old clock tower.

The fox watches from the alley, unseen. It has learned the rhythm of the town: which doors open, which dogs sleep, which bins overflow on which days.

Section 3: An unexpected detour

A delivery truck blocks the usual route, so the fox doubles back through the churchyard. Gravestones lean at angles, mossy and old, and a blackbird scolds from the yew tree.

Beyond the lychgate lies the allotment gardens, rich with the smell of turned earth, ripe tomatoes, and somebody's bonfire smoke drifting low.

Section 4: The allotments

Rows of potatoes, beans climbing canes, marrows swelling under big leaves. The fox pauses to listen: a robin, a distant lawnmower, the rattle of a watering can.

Nothing edible here today, so it slips under the fence and onto the footpath that follows the stream out of town.

Section 5: Along the stream

Water skaters dimple the surface. A heron stands statue-still, then strikes. Moorhens fuss among the reeds with their red-beaked chicks.

The path crosses a wooden bridge with a missing plank. The fox jumps it easily and continues into the water meadows.

Section 6: The water meadows

Butterflies drift over knapweed and scabious. The grass is tall enough to hide in, and the fox moves like a rumour through it.

At the far edge stands an oak, centuries old, hollow at the base. Inside the hollow: dry leaves, a beetle, and the smell of fox from winters past.

Section 7: Midday rest

The fox curls in the hollow and sleeps. Dreams of rabbits, of moonlit fields, of the vixen from two valleys over with the torn ear.

Flies hum. The oak leaves turn their silver undersides up, which means rain later. The fox's ear twitches but it does not wake.

Section 8: Afternoon rain

Rain arrives as promised, drumming on the canopy. Drips find the hollow's entrance and the fox shuffles deeper into the dry dark.

An hour passes. The rain softens to drizzle, then stops. Everything smells washed: leaf, bark, soil, and far off, wet sheep.

Section 9: The way back

Hunger returns. The fox retraces its steps at a trot, shaking water from its brush. The stream is higher now, brown and hurried.

At the allotments a gardener has left a sandwich on a bench. The fox considers it, then leaves it: too much bread, not enough chicken.

Section 10: Evening in town

Lamps come on. Bats flicker over the square. The bins behind the bakery are tonight's plan, and the plan is good.

Stale croissants, a pork pie with one bite taken, and a whole unsliced loaf, still soft. The fox eats like a king behind the bins.

Section 11: Night patrol

Fed, the fox tours its territory: the churchyard wall, the allotment fence, the bridge, the oak. It marks each point with care.

From the hill it looks back at the town's orange glow. Somewhere a dog barks once and gives up. An owl asks its single question of the dark.

Section 12: Home

The den is under the shed behind the last house. Dry, warm, smelling of earth and family. The fox circles three times and sleeps.
